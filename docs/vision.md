# LithoDrop: Vision & Design Manifesto

## The Premise

You are a Dropmaster — a contracted logistics specialist for an off-world colonization mega-corp. Your job is not glamorous. There are no laser battles, no alien diplomacy, no hero's journey. You pilot industrial delivery landers through hostile atmospheres, drop prefabricated base modules on alien surfaces, and keep the colony's power grid from going dark long enough to satisfy Corporate's ever-increasing quota.

The galaxy is a construction site. You're the foreman.

---

## The Two Souls of LithoDrop

LithoDrop is designed around a fundamental tension between two very different kinds of pleasure.

**The Descent is tension.** It is a physics puzzle that punishes complacency. The moment your thumb lifts off the thruster too early, inertia takes over, and the fragile glass dome you're carrying shatters on impact. The satisfaction of a perfect "suicide burn" — full thrust until the last possible second, then absolute silence as the landing legs kiss the ground — is pure, kinetic, earned.

**The Build is flow.** It is the satisfaction of Tetris, but with meaning. Every module you snap into the grid makes the colony more alive. The power lines glow brighter. The credit ticker ticks faster. The drones scurry with purpose. You built this.

The game oscillates between these two emotional registers deliberately. Tension is only satisfying when it gives way to flow. Flow is only precious when you know the next drop is waiting.

## Design Pillars

### 1. Physics That Communicates

Every piece of visual and audio feedback exists to give the player actionable telemetry without looking at a number.

The thruster plume turns white-hot when you're burning at maximum thrust. You don't need to look at the fuel gauge to know you're burning too much.

The velocity vector turns red before you hit the critical threshold. Your body recognizes the color before your brain reads it.

The structural groan of an overloaded column starts before it actually collapses. You have a one-second window to add a support strut.

**Rule:** If the player needs to read a number to understand what's happening, the game has failed.

### 2. Payload Aerodynamics as Character

Each module type is a different puzzle. This is the core of the game's replayability.

The Titanium Foundation is a brick. It ignores the wind and falls like it weighs 40 tonnes, because it does. You have to start your braking burn embarrassingly early.

The Solar Array is a kite. In crosswinds, it is a test of patience. You tilt into the wind, compensate for the drift, and accept that the descent will take twice as long as a heavy drop.

The Deep Core Drill is a dart. It wants to fall fast. The challenge is resisting the urge to let it — because the impact tolerance is lower than you'd expect.

**Rule:** The shape and mass of the payload defines the entire feeling of a descent. New module types should always introduce a new aerodynamic challenge.

### 3. The Base as a Living System

The base is not a static collection of blocks. It is a network under constant stress.

Power flows from generators to consumers through physical connections. Severing a connection with a misplaced module doesn't just display an error — it physically cuts power to everything downstream. The lights go out. The drones stop. The silence is louder than any alarm.

**Rule:** Every cause must have a visible, physical effect. The base must feel alive enough that its death is tangible.

### 4. Corporate as the Silent Antagonist

The Megacorp never speaks directly to the player. It communicates through increasing tax rates, impossible quotas, and the occasional "Special Delivery" contract that exists to test the limits of your competence.

The win condition — the Anchor Project — is not a reward. It's a corporate asset. You're not conquering a planet. You're delivering a package. The victory screen is a contract fulfillment notification.

**Rule:** The game should feel like work. The satisfaction comes not from being celebrated, but from being indispensable.

## Visual Identity

**Heavy Metal Vector Art.** Modules have chunky, function-over-form silhouettes. Everything looks like it was designed by industrial engineers, not product designers. Hydraulic pistons, exposed wiring channels, heat exchanger fins.

**Color as Function:**
- **Yellow** = Industrial / warning
- **Green** = Life Support / operational
- **Blue** = Science / data
- **Red** = Power / danger
- **Cyan** = Plasma / thruster / UI chrome

**The Void Palette.** Backgrounds are deep, near-black space tones (`#070B14`). Modules pop against this darkness. The base glows from within when it's healthy. It goes dark when it's dying.

---

## Audio Philosophy

Audio is telemetry. A player with their eyes on the lander should be able to hear when their fuel is critically low (the engine pitch drops and stutters), when a structure is about to collapse (metal groaning), and when the economy is in crisis (the ambient music strips back to a bare, dissonant drone).

The one exception to this rule is the magnetic snap sound. When a drone locks a new module into the grid — a heavy mechanical CLUNK followed by a crisp digital CHIME — it is the most rewarding moment in the game. It is the sound of progress.

---

## Difficulty Philosophy

LithoDrop does not have explicit difficulty settings. Difficulty is modulated through:

1. **Planetary selection.** Luna Prime is forgiving. Vulcanis is punishing.
2. **Payload selection.** The player always chooses what to drop next. Dropping the cheap, easy Foundation is always an option. Dropping the fragile, expensive Hydroponics Dome is a choice.
3. **The Drop Bounty system.** Aggressive, high-risk descents pay more. Conservative, fuel-efficient descents are safe but slow. The player's own ambition is the difficulty slider.

**Rule:** The game should never feel unfair. Every crash should feel like the player's mistake, not the game's.
